Sicily 1943
— which group was Kenneth's?

The family record says Kenneth Price was a paratrooper, wounded in the invasion of Sicily in July 1943, and in a hospital in England before that month was out. His headstone says something else entirely: 116th Infantry Regiment, Private. This page works the question the way the records allow — from the invasion's order of battle down to a single Army serial number — and separates what is established, what is inference, and what is disproved.

§1 The short answer

Only two United States parachute infantry regiments fought in Sicily in July 1943 — the 504th and the 505th, both of Maj. Gen. Matthew B. Ridgway's 82nd Airborne Division. They jumped on two nights only: 9–10 July and 11 July.

If the Sicily wounding and the parachutist wings in Kenneth's profile are true, then on 9–11 July 1943 he was in the 82nd Airborne Division — most plausibly the 505th PIR, the 504th PIR (any battalion), or one of the small attached parachute support units: the 456th or 376th Parachute Field Artillery Battalion, Company B or C of the 307th Airborne Engineer Battalion, or a regimental/divisional medical or signal detachment. Inference · high confidence

Nothing in the family profile as it stands discriminates between the 504th and the 505th. Only a record carrying his name or serial number — a morning report, a hospital admission card, a roster — can do that. That search is already underway; see §7.

And one thing can be closed outright: he was not wounded with the 116th Infantry in Sicily. In July 1943 that regiment was in Devon and Cornwall, England, and it did not fire a shot in combat until Omaha Beach, 6 June 1944. The 116th on the stone belongs to a different chapter of his service. 116th-in-Sicily: disproved

§2 The two lifts

Operation Husky opened with four airborne operations — two British, two American. The American ones were flown by the 52nd Troop Carrier Wing out of airfields around Kairouan, Tunisia, where the 82nd had staged after landing at Casablanca on 10 May 1943. Established

The 505th jumps first

Night of 9–10 July 1943 · Drop set for 2346 hrs, 9 July
Force505th Regimental Combat Team, Col. James M. Gavin commanding — the first combat jump in U.S. Army history
Composition505th PIR (1st, 2nd, 3rd Battalions); 3rd Battalion, 504th PIR attached; 456th Parachute Field Artillery Battalion; Company B, 307th Airborne Engineer Battalion; a signal detail and regimental medical detachments
Strength3,405 paratroopers in 226 C-47s
Drop zonesAround Piano Lupo / Ponte Olivo airfield, north-east of Gela, astride the Niscemi–Gela road; the 3/504 zone (DZ “Q”) lay south of Niscemi
ObjectivesSeize the high ground and road net inland from the 1st Infantry Division's Gela beaches; take Objective Y — sixteen concrete pillboxes commanding the roads; block the Hermann Göring Panzer Division's reserves
What happened35-mph winds and navigation failures scattered the sticks across south-eastern Sicily, some into the British sector. The scattered groups still cut communications, took the pillboxes, and on 10–11 July held Biazza Ridge against German armour until the 1st Division relieved them. 1st Battalion commander Lt. Col. Arthur F. Gorham was killed.

A caution carried by the research: some secondary summaries attach the 376th Parachute Field Artillery to Gavin's team. The 505th's own after-action report and Phil Nordyke's divisional history agree it was the 456th here, and the 376th with the 504th two nights later — the AAR wins.

The 504th — and the friendly-fire disaster

Night of 11 July 1943 · Farello airfield, east of Gela
Force504th Regimental Combat Team, Col. Reuben H. Tucker commanding
Composition504th PIR less its 3rd Battalion (already in with the 505th) — Regimental HQ, 1st and 2nd Battalions, service and medical elements; 376th Parachute Field Artillery Battalion; Company C, 307th Airborne Engineer Battalion. Brig. Gen. Charles L. Keerans Jr., the assistant division commander, flew as an observer — and was killed.
Strength144 C-47s carrying roughly 1,900–2,305 paratroopers (sources differ on what they count)
Drop zoneFarello airfield / the Gela–Farello area, about five miles inland from Gela — the ground the first lift was to have secured for them

The disaster. The mission was ordered and launched the same day, after an Axis air raid on the Gela anchorage. Word never reached the whole fleet, no safe corridor had been cleared, and as the formation crossed the coast an Allied naval vessel opened fire — then naval and shore anti-aircraft guns joined in. 23 of 144 C-47s were shot down and 37 more heavily damaged; eight turned back without dropping. Men were shot in their harnesses or dropped into the sea. By morning only about 400 of the regiment had assembled on the objective; the rest fought as isolated groups. Tucker's own aircraft reached the drop zone riddled with holes.

The casualty figures need their caveat, and the research carries one: commonly quoted totals run to 318 American casualties (83 dead) for the lift as a whole; the 504th itself is given as 81 dead, 132 wounded, 16 missing, with the 52nd Troop Carrier Wing's aircrew adding 7 dead, 30 wounded, 53 missing. Authors differ on whether aircrew and missing-later-declared-dead are counted. The order of magnitude — eighty-odd dead, and between 130 and 230 paratroopers wounded — is consistent across sources. Established, as a range

After the jumps the fighting went on: from 13 July the 504th spearheaded the 82nd's drive north-west along the south coast, and the division took the Marsala–Trapani area by 23 July. So a paratrooper could have been wounded on Sicily any time from 10 July into early August — but Kenneth's profile has him already in England in late July, which points to a wound in the first half of the month: the jumps themselves, or the Gela and Biazza Ridge fighting. Inference

§3 Who didn't jump — the exclusions that narrow the field

Half the value of the order of battle is negative. Every unit below was real, airborne, and somewhere else — which is why the candidate list for Kenneth collapses to two regiments and their attachments.

In reserve · Tunisia

325th Glider Infantry Regiment

Held back with the 80th Airborne Antiaircraft Battalion. Its glider landing, set for the night of 11–12 July, was cancelled by Ridgway after the Husky II disaster. It was flown into Sicily only on 6–7 September, as a non-assault move. Some lineage summaries credit it with “landed on Sicily 9 July 1943” — the division's own narrative contradicts that shorthand. There was no U.S. glider assault in Sicily.

In reserve · Tunisia

509th Parachute Infantry Battalion

Attached to the 82nd but likewise in reserve in Tunisia through the western-Sicily drive. Its next jump was Avellino, Italy, on 14 September 1943 — not Sicily.

Elsewhere entirely

Every other parachute regiment

The 501st and 502nd (101st Airborne) were still in the United States, sailing for England in September 1943. The 503rd was in the Pacific. The 506th, 507th and 508th were in the U.S. — the 507th and 508th didn't reach England until late 1943 / early 1944. The 511th, 517th and others were still forming. The Army's own account credits the American drops solely to the 82nd.

The British airborne operations — glider-borne Operation Ladbroke against the Ponte Grande bridge on 9–10 July, and Operation Fustian at Primosole Bridge on 13–14 July — complete the picture of Husky's four airborne operations, but no American soldier jumped with them.

§4 The road from Camp Wolters

The family record has Kenneth training “with a parachute battalion at Camp Wolters, Texas,” in 1942. That detail, as stated, matches nothing — and the correction turns out to strengthen the case rather than weaken it.

Camp Wolters, four miles east of Mineral Wells, Texas, was the Army's largest Infantry Replacement Training Center — basic infantry training, with a troop capacity that peaked at 24,973. No source found describes any parachute battalion, jump tower, or parachute training there; its battalions were IRTC battalions turning out trained infantry replacements. Established

All parachute qualification in 1942 happened at Fort Benning, Georgia, where the Parachute School was formally activated on 15 May 1942. Paratroopers were volunteers, and replacement training centers were a recruiting ground: a trainee could volunteer during or after basic, go to Benning, earn his wings, and be posted to a parachute regiment needing fillers. The pipeline is documented with a named example — Angel Romero volunteered at Camp Wolters, trained at Fort Benning, and was assigned to G Company, 508th PIR. Established pattern

The timing is the point. The 82nd was the only airborne division to deploy overseas in the first half of 1943. A man winged at Benning between September 1942 and March 1943 and posted to a regiment at Fort Bragg would overwhelmingly have joined the 504th or the 505th — the only parachute units whose Sicily jump he could then have made. Men winged in the same months also went to the 506th, 507th and 508th, but none of those was in Sicily, so if the wounding is real, those postings are excluded for Kenneth. The August 1942 marriage fits either regiment's timeline without strain: both were at Fort Benning and Fort Bragg through that autumn. Inference from established dates

So “parachute battalion at Camp Wolters” is best read as a conflation — a photograph or caption from his IRTC battalion at Wolters, retold alongside wings earned afterward at Benning. It is the single most likely point of distortion in the family documents, and the research flags it as such. Inference · high confidence

§5 The wounded man's road — Sicily to England

Kenneth's profile puts him in a hospital in England from the latter part of July 1943. Is that consistent with a Sicily wound on 9–11 July? Yes — but it was not the typical route, and the exception itself is a clue.

The typical chain

Battalion aidSicily→ Clearing / evacuation hospitalSicily, as units landed→ Air or hospital shipfrom 14 July, Gela / Licata→ Base hospitalsMateur · Tunis · Bizerte — 8,250 beds→ Ship direct to the U.S.for cases over 90 days

Between 10 July and 10 August 1943, 5,967 patients were evacuated by air to Mateur and Tunis alone. Men expected to recover quickly stayed in theater; men facing more than ninety days in hospital were put aboard U.S.-bound transports — which is how most long-term Sicily wounded reached a stateside general hospital like O'Reilly without passing through England at all. Established

A worked example from Kenneth's own candidate units: T/5 Charles E. Hinchen, Company B, 307th Airborne Engineer Battalion — one of the attached units on the candidate list — was lightly wounded at Biazza Ridge on 11 July 1943. His morning-report chain runs: wounded → 7th Station Hospital → 21st General Hospital (2 Aug 1943) → hospital in the Zone of Interior, i.e. the U.S. (6 Sept 1943). His “general hospital” stage was the 21st at Sidi Bou Hanifia, Algeria — North Africa, not England.

The England route — real, but a minority

The 298th General Hospital at Frenchay Park, Bristol, records that “starting in the spring of 1943, the patients first received were evacuated from North Africa, Sicily, and Italy.” During 1943 it received 573 battle casualties — three out of four of them complicated fractures of knees, elbows and ankles needing long-term treatment (about 80% of them British; British policy sent the most serious cases from each hospital ship there). The Army Medical Department's consultant history treats American wounded evacuated from North Africa to U.K. hospitals as a defined cohort, typically arriving about two to three weeks after being wounded. Established

Judgement: a man wounded 9–11 July, cleared to Bizerte or Tunis within days, and selected — most plausibly for a complicated extremity fracture needing long, specialist treatment — for a hospital ship sailing to a British west-coast port could be in an English hospital in the last week of July, exactly matching the documented transit time. The 298th General Hospital at Frenchay Park is the single best-documented candidate hospital. Inference

No source found names another English hospital receiving Sicily casualties in July 1943 — that is a stated gap in the research, not a negative proof. And two cautions ride along: family memory might compress a North-Africa hospital stage plus a later pass through England into “hospital in England”; but if a hospital admission card does place him in England in late July, it corroborates the Sicily wound rather than contradicting it — no U.S. ground combat was producing American battle casualties anywhere near England that month.

O'Reilly General Hospital, Springfield, Missouri, fits as the stateside terminus: built for long-term care of returning soldiers, dedicated 8 November 1941 at 1,000 beds (later 3,500), and a centre for reconstructive surgery and physical therapy — the expected destination for a man evacuated home for prolonged treatment. Established

§6 The 116th problem

The headstone and obituary name the 116th Infantry Regiment, at the rank of Private. A hometown newspaper instead called him a Technical Sergeant at the Shenango Personnel Replacement Depot in Pennsylvania. The regiment's own record settles where it was — and it wasn't Sicily.

The 116th, a Virginia National Guard regiment of the 29th Infantry Division, crossed on the Queen Mary in September 1942 and reached the United Kingdom on 5 October 1942. It trained at Tidworth, then in late May 1943 moved with the division to Devon and Cornwall — coast defence around Plymouth, hard training on Dartmoor and Bodmin Moor, and in July 1943 the start of amphibious assault training at Slapton Sands. Its first combat was the first wave at Omaha Beach on 6 June 1944. Established

No man was wounded in action with the 116th Infantry Regiment in Sicily in July 1943. The regiment had never left England since October 1942 and holds no Sicily campaign credit. So at least one of the two unit attributions — paratrooper in Sicily, or 116th Infantry — belongs to a different period of Kenneth's service. The records system of 1943 makes that not just possible but ordinary: a man evacuated beyond his division was struck from his company's rolls, and on recovery re-entered the Army as an individual replacement, liable to be posted anywhere.

The reconciliations, ranked

  • Post-recovery reassignment via Shenango — most plausible. After O'Reilly, a recovered (or limited-duty) soldier bound overseas again would be staged through a Personnel Replacement Depot. Shenango — renamed Camp Reynolds on 21 September 1943 — received, processed and forwarded over a million troops toward East Coast ports and the European theater. From a U.K. replacement depot he could be posted to a division in England short of men — and the 116th in Devon was exactly that. A paratrooper who had lost jump status through his wound was, to that system, simply an infantry replacement. On this reading the newspaper catches him in transit, and the stone's 116th is his last unit — the one he was discharged from.
  • The stone was drawn from a later record. The VA does not research headstones: the applicant fills out VA Form 40-1330 from the veteran's discharge or other supporting documents, and the inscription follows what is submitted. If his final discharge paper — a later separation, or a post-war enlistment — named the 116th, the stone names the 116th, even if all his combat service was airborne. An obituary written from the stone, or from the same paper, would repeat “116th” with no independent value.
  • The rank conflict — Technical Sergeant vs. Private — stays open. The two labels may date from different documents at different times (a man can hold one grade in an assignment and be discharged at another, after a reduction, a break in service, or a fresh enlistment as a Private); the newspaper may have erred, as hometown papers often did; or the stone simply carries the rank on the single discharge document the family submitted, which need not be his highest wartime grade. Only dated personnel records resolve it. Unresolved

One dating clue hides in the newspaper item itself: if it says “Shenango Personnel Replacement Depot,” it was most likely written before the 21 September 1943 renaming to Camp Reynolds — or copied from an address he used before then. If the item is from mid-to-late 1943, the whole arc (Sicily wound in July → England → O'Reilly → Shenango) compresses into a single year and should be checked against the paper's actual date; if it's from 1944, the looser timeline is comfortable. The research did not have the newspaper's date. Inference

Readings the research states and discards: that he jumped in Sicily with the 116th (impossible, above); that the 116th attribution is pure invention (unnecessary — the replacement pipeline explains it); and that two different soldiers have been conflated (possible in any family file, but there is no positive evidence for it, and the coherent single-man timeline should be tested first).

§7 The proof trail — and the lead already in hand

This question is answerable. The decisive records are name-and-serial-number records, and the family's own search of them has already produced a hit.

The morning-report lead

Army serial number

17057680

Searching the NARA Catalog's morning reports by that serial number alone returns thirteen reels that carry it — the number genuinely walks those pages. Six of the thirteen sit in the Sicily window:

Reel 381 Reel 2785 Reel 2824 + 3 more reels · Jan 1940 – Jul 1943 7 reels · Oct 1943 – Apr 1944

The later reels could be Kenneth's own paper trail — hospital transfers, the Shenango depot days, a furlough — or a clerk's similar digits fooling the machine's eye; only the pages themselves will tell. (A name search returns twenty-one more reels.) The Catalog shows the reel, never the page, and each reel holds hundreds of report images — so the next step is plain: open the three Sicily-window reels first and read until his status-change lines appear. A wounded man's entries — wounded in action, to hospital, transferred — are exactly the lines that name his company and regiment.

Jump manifests — a caution Troop-carrier mission reports and serial tables survive with the AARs and at the Air Force Historical Research Agency — but no complete name-by-name jump manifest for Husky I or II is known to survive. Treat any website claiming a complete Husky passenger list with scepticism until it cites a serial or mission file.

§9 Open questions

  1. The man himself is not on the open web. No source naming Kenneth Price in connection with the 504th, 505th, 116th, Camp Wolters, or a Sicily wound was found; a targeted name search returned no match. His company, home state, and the exact dates of the newspaper item and of his O'Reilly and Shenango stays are the missing keys the research did not have — the serial-number search in §7 is now supplying the sharpest of them.
  2. Which English hospital, and on which sailing? The 298th General Hospital is a documented candidate, not a confirmed admission. No July 1943 admission list for Sicily casualties in England was found online.
  3. Husky II's numbers move by source — 1,900 vs. 2,000 vs. 2,305 men; 81 vs. 83 dead; 229 vs. 318 casualties — depending on whether aircrew, attached troops and the missing are counted. This page reports the range rather than one figure.
  4. Two source conflicts are flagged, not hidden. Some lineage summaries say the 325th “landed in Sicily 9 July 1943,” against the division's own narrative; and some secondary summaries swap the 376th and 456th Parachute Field Artillery between the two lifts. In both cases the after-action reports and the division narrative were preferred.
