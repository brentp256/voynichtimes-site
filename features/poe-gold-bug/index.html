Mystery Puzzle History · Vol. 1, Part 1

Mystery Puzzle Games and Their Origin From… Poe?

Or, what was the first Mystery Puzzle Game?

Mystery Puzzle Games (MPG) is one of several terms used to describe an explosively growing hobby full of armchair detectives, ghost hunters, treasure seekers, and solvers of puzzles and mysteries — a hobby that traces its origins back to Edgar Allan Poe, with roots leading further into ancient cryptography, detective fiction, and the history of puzzles. Other names are viable, but for consistency these articles will use Mystery Puzzle Game.

Hard to define

Defining this hobby can be as simple as knowing it when you see it, and as difficult as slicing through multiple overlapping Venn diagrams. There is disagreement about where the lines are, which circles create those diagrams, and what actually belongs here, along with many cousin lines and offshoots (escape rooms, puzzle boxes, and the like).

Many hobbies are defined by the people inside them. This one was created through multiple means, times, and methods, without even the forethought of being part of a larger, specific hobby. Some makers just wanted a more immersive murder mystery. Others wanted to add storylines or themes to puzzles. Others wanted immersive worlds and experiences. Some created books; some added physical props to books; others built subscription boxes of paperwork and artifacts; still others moved to digital platforms. With all of that variety, how do we define the hobby?

The largest players — and a few neighbors

One of the best ways to describe the genre is to start with some of the largest players in the field (circa early 2020s) and work outward:

  • Hunt a Killer
  • Deadbolt Mystery Society
  • Murder Mystery in a Box
  • Mysterious Package Company

From there, many newcomers have entered the market. A small sample:

  • The Curious Correspondence Club
  • The Scarlet Envelope
  • The Boundless Library
  • The Society of Curiosities

Another type is puzzle books, from companies such as:

  • Escapages
  • Escape Book
  • Montague Island Mysteries

And those are only some of the hundreds of known samples.

What it is not

What defines this hobby and separates it from similar ones? One way in is to look at what it is not — though that line is often fuzzy. The Venn diagram overlaps several other hobbies, not surprisingly.

  • Escape rooms (a close cousin, and often a progenitor of this hobby)
    • In person
    • Virtual
  • ARGs (alternate reality games) — another cousin
  • Whodunnit detective stories — a really close sibling that crosses the line at times
  • Board games (with some exceptions)
  • Card games (with some exceptions)

Confusing, isn’t it?

Mystery + Puzzle + Game

The key elements of a Mystery Puzzle Game are the three concentric circles already in the name:

  1. Mystery
  2. Puzzle
  3. Game

It is a game, first and foremost: the reader or player needs to absorb information and act on it — versus passively reading a novel, for example.

It contains a mystery to solve. That is the primary action the player must complete. It can be as simple as guessing the killer from evidence provided, or as layered as uncovering mystery and meaning through hidden clues across multiple products.

It is a puzzle. The mystery is not merely presented; it must be actively solved, whether through deductive reasoning, decoding ciphers, researching real-world locations for clues, or drawing a map and folding a piece of paper.

A Mystery Puzzle Game is a game where a player solves a mystery through puzzles.

There are many exceptions, but that center line frames this article and the ones that follow.

Why Poe?

How did this all begin, and what does Edgar Allan Poe have to do with it? He didn’t create games, never sent boxes to players at home, and had no concept of this genre existing. So what makes him the keystone?

First, a timeline of the earliest era of the hobby — coined here as “The Progenitor Age,” because many of these works are not fully realized Mystery Puzzle Games, but evolutionary steps toward them.

  • 1843 — The Gold-Bug (cipher in a story)
  • 1881 — The Cryptogram, book 2 of Eight Hundred Leagues on the Amazon by Jules Verne (descendant of The Gold-Bug)
  • 1907 (June 30) — New York Tribune detective puzzle (Possibly the first…)
  • 1926 — The Haverfordian publishes John Dickson Carr’s The Shadow of the Goat, a short story that lets the reader try to solve the mystery before the next month’s answer (We argue it is the first Mystery Puzzle Game)
  • 1928 — Cipher Stories Puzzle Book (probably Feb) — twenty-five original short stories, each containing at least two vital cipher puzzles
  • 1928 — November: The Baffle Book published (Wren, McKay)
  • 1929 — October: the second Baffle Book published (Wren, McKay)
  • 1930 — October: the third Baffle Book published (Wren, McKay)
  • 1932 — Minute Mysteries published (Ripley)
  • 1933 — The Mystery Puzzle Book published (Wren, McKay)
  • 1935 — Photocrimes published in Britain in Weekly Illustrated
  • 1936 — Crimefile No. 1: The File on Bolitho Blane
  • 1936 — September 20: Photocrimes in the Des Moines Register — the earliest U.S. example we have discovered so far
  • 1936 — November 30: Photocrimes book published
  • 1936 — The Marceau Case (dossier novel)
  • 1936 — X. Jones of Scotland Yard (dossier novel)
  • 1937 — Crimefile No. 2: File on Rufus Ray
  • 1937 — File on Robert Prentice
  • 1937 — Photocrimes
  • 1937 — Crimefile No. 3: File on Fenton and Farr
  • 1938 — Crimefile No. 4: File on Claudia Cragge
  • 1938 — The Malinsay Massacre
  • 1939 — Herewith the Clues
  • 1940s — Photocrimes in the Des Moines Register, using work from David Nowinson (creator of “You Solve the Crime”) and Harold Ripley (creator of Minute Mysteries)

Future articles will discuss these works in more detail. For now: how did Poe become the first data point?

The three concentric circles leading into this hobby, again, are mysteries, puzzles, and games.

Mysteries (Detectives, Locked Room)

Mysteries have been around for a long time. Most mystery puzzle games dig into that history, often through detective-style work. Edgar Allan Poe is widely credited with inventing the modern detective story, beginning with Dupin. Dupin would lead directly to Holmes, and from there to Agatha Christie, Henri Bencolin, and dozens of others. Detective fiction exploded in the 1930s, leading to hundreds of books and magazines and a craze for figuring out how crimes were done as you read.

The Murders in the Rue Morgue is a foundational locked-room mystery in detective fiction — a death in a locked room with no apparent way the victim could have been murdered. The locked-room mystery is also a progenitor of computer escape-the-room games, which would lead to True Dungeon at Gen Con in 2003 and then to the modern escape-room hobby — topics for a future article.

Puzzle (cryptography, Solve-it-Yourself)

Cryptography has an ancient and integral place in history. It was not popularized, though, until Poe championed it in his works and articles. That path leads to his 1843 story The Gold-Bug, which centers on a cipher presented so the reader could have solved it before reading the very detailed solution in the story. It was not presented as a game, per se, but as a way to educate the reader about cryptography.

Content note: Period editions include racial depictions and stereotypes that do not conform to modern sensibilities. We cite the story as a historical seed of the hobby, not as a model for modern design.

While The Gold-Bug is not technically a solve-it-yourself book, the cipher portion is a step toward that genre — and toward a foundation of this hobby. It is a direct ancestor of Jules Verne’s The Cryptogram, book 2 of Eight Hundred Leagues on the Amazon. Then, in 1907 (June 30), a Detective Puzzle was published in the New York Tribune (Possibly the first…). These were visual puzzles for children, with prompts explaining the setup and “clews” (as they were called). They are fairly simple for adults, but they are a fun example of detective-themed puzzles and show the influence detective fiction would continue to have on the game hobby.

Game

Games have been around since ancient times. The history most related to this hobby begins in 1913 with the first “Word-Cross” in the New York World, which led to an explosion of adults in the 1920s working out crosswords (and a few similar puzzles) at home, while their kids focused on Detective Puzzles and other simplified-for-children puzzles.

By the late 1920s, the seeds had been planted and were starting to grow — ready to explode in the 1930s in the first wave of Mystery Puzzle Games. The culmination of paths leading from ancient times, through Poe, and through the idea of publishing puzzles that could be solved at home (e.g., the crossword) arrives in 1926.

The Shadow of the Goat (1926)

In 1926, The Haverfordian published The Shadow of the Goat, a short story by John Dickson Carr, in their college magazine. It allowed the reader a chance to solve the mystery before the answer appeared the following month. It was an “impossible crime”: on the surface, like a locked-door murder, it could not have happened.

The story took the detective crime fiction begun with Poe and continued by many others, then added a game element. Instead of a reader trying to solve it as they went along, there was a purposeful break: the reader is asked to solve it before the answer arrived in the next issue. (We argue it is the first Mystery Puzzle Game.) In infancy, yes — but already a story, clues, and a puzzle (in this case, a whodunnit).
