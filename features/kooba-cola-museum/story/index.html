The Story

Kooba Cola, with Vitamin B-1 — the cola that almost was, told whole: the invention, the campaign, the two launches that were real, the end, and the afterlife.

1. A soda invented in a comic book

Kooba Cola was the brainchild of Victor S. Fox — comic-book magnate, publisher of Swank, and, in the museum's founding judgment, a man who believed you could create demand for a product that did not exist and let the demand pull the product into being behind it. The paper came first. The Kooba trademark was filed with the U.S. Patent Office on August 20, 1940 and registered on January 21, 1941 (Registration No. 384,537). The Kooba Corporation kept its offices at 247 Park Avenue, New York — telephone PLaza 8-0100 — with cap redemptions mailed to 125 East 46th Street and coupons routed through the Fox Publications offices at 480 Lexington Avenue. The campaign was placed through the Harry A. Berk agency.

What Fox had, in the fall of 1940, was distribution no soda startup could buy: his own comic books, millions of copies a month, read by exactly the children a five-cent cola with prizes would be aimed at. What he did not yet have, anywhere, was soda.

2. Fall 1940 — the ads arrive first

The first Kooba ads reached newsstands in the summer of 1940, in Fox comics cover-dated September and October. The lead creative — the museum's KC1, "Refresh With Kooba" — promised the whole proposition in one page: a cola drink with Vitamin B-1, "tangy and cooling as an ocean breeze," in a giant 12-ounce bottle for 5¢, "enough for two," six-bottle cartons at 25¢. Vitamin B-1 had only just become available and was making newspaper headlines; Fox sold his cola as a tonic that happened to taste good.

The covers joined in. Teaser bursts on Fantastic Comics, Mystery Men Comics, Wonderworld Comics and Samson promised a "FREE BOTTLE OF KOOBA — See Inside," and inside sat the coupon ad (KC3): "Boys... Girls... Everybody!!" Clip the coupon, present it to the dealer who sold you the magazine, and receive a big 12-ounce bottle absolutely free, the offer running August 15 to November 15, 1940.

The fine print told the truer story. If your dealer "happens to be out of KOOBA or has yet to be supplied," you were to mail the coupon to New York instead, and Fox would send "an attractive and interesting gift" as a substitute. A national free-soda campaign whose fallback was a mail-in gift is a campaign that expected, in most of the country, to have no soda to give. The coupons were a measuring instrument: they told Fox where the demand was, dealer by dealer, town by town. (The full run of these pieces hangs in the Gallery, Rooms I–III.)

3. The premium machine

Kooba's second engine was the bottle cap. Caps worked "the same as money": save them, mail them in, and pick from a premium catalog of toys, cameras, baseball outfits, even a radio set. The catalog itself was advertised in two-page spreads inside the comics — the soda, the caps, the prizes and the mascot, the Kooba Kid, all in one spread. The Kid got his own strip ads and a limerick contest. A Treasure Hunt contest followed, and this one demonstrably paid out: the winners' page ran in Big 3 #4 in July 1941 (Gallery, Room IV).

It was a complete promotional machine — coupons to measure demand, caps to drive repeat purchase, contests to keep the name in front of children between issues — built and running at full speed before most of America could have bought a bottle at any price.

4. Honolulu — the launch that was real

Kooba reached Hawaii in December 1940. The Honolulu bottler and distributor was the Malolo Soda Works, 1340 N. School St., Honolulu — proprietor Chang Chow, manager Alex Y. Leong, a firm then in business for 15 years. Kooba was sold there only in the large 12-ounce bottle, at 5 cents, and drinkers were told to save the caps: a premium catalog was available at dealers and at the Malolo factory.

The launch ran in both Honolulu dailies on December 16, 1940 — a news article in each paper ("Kooba Is Here Today" in the Star-Advertiser; "'Kooba' Contest Will Mark Debut Of Cola Drink" in the Star-Bulletin) and a full launch ad built around a sentence contest: complete "I LIKE KOOBA BECAUSE…" in 20 words or less and send it, with one Kooba cap or facsimile, to Malolo Soda Works by December 24. Prizes were $15 in cash ($7.50 / $5.00 / $2.50) plus 20 cases of Kooba, one case each to the next twenty. The launch ad printed Malolo's telephone number wrong — 4149 instead of 8149 — and both papers carried corrections on December 17 and 18, by which time the contest was already drawing, in the Star-Bulletin's words, "a hearty response." The ads ran through Christmas (a holiday greeting from Malolo on December 25), and the winners were announced in the Star-Advertiser on January 12, 1941: first prize to William L. Dye, second to W. F. Roeschal, third to Miss Lela Linn, with twenty more winners taking a case each. Dye's winning line: "I like Kooba because when my appetite wheedles 'thirsty' and my health conscience warns 'careful' I can always depend on Kooba to satisfy both."

Two hundred entries, the papers said. In Honolulu, for one winter, the whole machine — the ads, the contest, the caps, the prizes — ran exactly as designed, on a soda you could actually buy. The Honolulu pages hang in the Gallery, Room VII, and Malolo's entry is in The Bottlers.

5. New York — the trade push

At the start of 1941 the corporation expanded like a company going somewhere. A broker's report in the New York Post of January 20, 1941 records the Colonial News Co. and its affiliated enterprises — Fox Feature Syndicate, Fox Radio Features and Kooba Corp., "headed by Victor S. Fox" — leasing the eighth floor at 247 Park Avenue, supplementing the ninth floor next door at 480 Lexington. Two months later the Brooklyn Daily Eagle carried the trade ad: "KOOBA COMES TO BROOKLYN," bottled fresh daily at a new Brooklyn plant at 705 Driggs Avenue, recruiting dealers, wholesalers and jobbers off the comics' combined national circulation of 2,000,000 copies a month.

Through the spring the want ads multiplied across Long Island and Queens — salesmen wanted in Nassau–Suffolk, an individual distributor in Astoria recruiting his own men, the Fresco Beverage Bottling Company of Long Island City hiring, a Jamaica outfit looking for distributors with trucks of their own. The comics had built the demand. In the spring of 1941, in New York at least, the supply side was genuinely trying to catch up. (Gallery, Room VIII.)

6. Miami — Fox bottles his own

The second real bottling operation was Fox's own. The Kooba Bottling Company of Miami, Inc. was chartered on November 18, 1940 — with Victor S. Fox himself listed as a director — and bottled Kooba at 1333 NE Bayshore Place through 1941. A Miami newspaper coupon from that year (a dime off a 25¢ six-bottle carton, the offer expiring June 28, 1941) survives because of what happened to it twenty-four years later; see the Afterlife, below.

Miami is also where the end shows first. Liquidation ads ran in December 1941, the last traces fade in the spring of 1942, and the company was dissolved in 1945. The site at 1333 NE Bayshore Place was later occupied by the Miami Herald's own building — which is how a Herald columnist came to be holding a Kooba coupon in 1965.

7. The end of the line

The campaign's last season was the summer of 1941: the free-bottle Sampling Week in July, the Treasure Hunt winners, and in August the Kooba Girl's most famous appearance, the "Thirsty?" pin-up in Swank. After that the record thins to the Miami liquidation and silence. Kooba had run for about a year as advertising and for a few months, in a few places, as a drink. The trademark, the ads and one bottle cap outlasted the soda by decades.

8. Afterlife

On February 18, 1965, the Miami Herald's Larry Thompson devoted his column to a reader's find: a Kooba coupon, expired June 28, 1941, turned up in Miami, and Thompson reconstructed what he could — "Kooba was put out by the Kooba Bottling Co. of Miami, 1333 NE Bayshore Pl." — before admitting, "I don't know what happened to Kooba." The column hangs last in the Gallery (Room IX). It is the first piece of Kooba historiography, written twenty-four years after the fact, in the soda's own hometown paper.

In 2021 a collector found a used Kooba bottle cap — blue and white, cork-lined, its crimped edge stamped "Eckrite" — among other circa-1940 caps. It remains the only documented physical Kooba artifact known: proof in stamped tin that the bottles were real. It hangs in the Gallery, Room I.

This museum is the afterlife's current address. The watch desk scans the marketplaces daily; every genuine Kooba artifact that surfaces is catalogued — the full working catalog, all 201 entries, is published in The Catalog — and one piece is featured every day on the museum's channels.
