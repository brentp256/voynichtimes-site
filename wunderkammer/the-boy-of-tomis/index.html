The Boy of Tomis

A sealed sarcophagus, two clay toys, and the city that exiled Ovid

Thursday, October 29, 2026

Constanța, Romania. On Thursday, October 29, the word from Constanța is a good one: an intact Roman sarcophagus has been opened, and inside was a most exciting find — a boy, his treasures, and a story. The Museum of National History and Archaeology Constanța (MINAC) puts the tomb at nearly 1,800 years old. Its formal dating is not out yet. The museum has named him Băiețelul de la Tomis — the Little Boy from Tomis.

Constanța is built on top of Tomis, and digging there is not a project with an end date. For years the museum's archaeologists have worked the site in pieces, usually ahead of construction — earlier this year they finished excavating 34 Roman tombs under the municipal hospital site, work that ran from September 2025 to February 2026. The dead of Tomis turn up under hospitals, streets, and apartment plots, and the necropolis on the city's northern edge is still giving them up.

Then, on Tuesday, October 27, digging in the Flămânda district, on that northern edge of the necropolis, they found the sarcophagus. On Wednesday they opened it, and livestreamed the opening. Inside was a child — a boy, the museum believes — buried with his personal ornaments, a medallion, one coin, two clay toys, and flower garlands. Coverage of the opening quoted the team on the preservation: even the hair is preserved.

Most of the tombs were robbed

Most tombs at Tomis were robbed in antiquity. The cross-shaped hypogeum found under a plot on Sabinelor Street, not far from this find, was looted before Rome fell. A sealed sarcophagus was never entered, so everything inside is still where it was placed at the funeral. That is rarer than any single object in it, and it is what makes this more than a good find: the full arrangement survived, so researchers can study the burial as it was made, not as looters left it.

Ovid's city

Tomis is where the emperor Augustus exiled the poet Ovid in AD 8, for what Ovid called “a poem and a mistake.” He lived there about ten years, wrote the Tristia and the Letters from the Black Sea (Epistulae ex Ponto) there, and died there around AD 17 without being recalled. He complained about the place the whole time — the cold, the frozen wine, the fighting at the walls. The city keeps his statue in Piața Ovidiu, the square named after him, and the museum that opened this sarcophagus stands on that square and keeps a room devoted to him. The boy was buried in the necropolis of that same city, in the Roman centuries after Ovid's death. So far as we know, nobody has connected the two before this week. There is nothing to connect, exactly — just the same few streets, holding both of them.

What was in the sarcophagus

  • The coin. Coins in graves are often read as Charon's fee — the fare for the ferryman who rows the dead across the Styx. Scholars argue about how literally any given family meant that. Either way, a coin in a burial was placed there on purpose, and once the museum reads this one it may date the tomb more tightly than anything else in it.
  • The two clay toys. Not valuables. They are the part of the burial that belonged to the boy's life rather than his family's standing, and they are the detail every report on this find has led with, for understandable reasons.
  • The garlands. Flowers laid at the funeral, and the most perishable things in the box. They survived in traces because the sarcophagus stayed sealed — no air, no looters, no disturbance.
  • The medallion. Romanian coverage calls it mysterious. We would call it unread, so far. The museum's analysis is the thing to watch for.
  • The ornaments. Personal adornments buried with him, not yet described in detail in the coverage we have seen.

The wider necropolis

This is not a lone find. Earlier this year the same museum excavated 34 Roman tombs under the municipal hospital site in Constanța — third- and fourth-century burials, catacombs reached by a tile stair that survived intact, a Greek inscription naming a religious association in Tomis, and the umbo (the center boss) of a parade shield, which the museum calls extremely rare. Tomis keeps turning up under the modern city: under hospitals, streets, and apartment plots. The Boy of Tomis is one burial out of thousands in that necropolis. He is the one who reached us unopened.

Observations

We spend most of our time on mysteries somebody built on purpose — ciphers, dossiers, buried treasure with a poem for a map. Nobody built this one. There is nothing here to solve, and we are running it anyway, because this is what evidence looks like before anyone turns it into a mystery: a sealed box, an arrangement, and a short list of objects that have to be explained one at a time. The follow-up is the part we will be watching — the coin's date, the medallion, and the museum's full report.
