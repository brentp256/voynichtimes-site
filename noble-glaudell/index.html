Noble Glaudell was born on February 16, 1907, in East St. Louis, Illinois, the son of Lee Sebastian Glaudell and Cora Rainford. His mother died when he was nine, and he grew up on Will County farmland in his mother's people's care. In 1934 he married Adeline Schaal; the marriage ended, and he went to war as a signalman with the 3165th Signal Service Company. After the war he married June Hennings, worked as a government inspector, and spent his last years in Reedsburg, Wisconsin, where he died on August 5, 1972.

§1 The boy from East St. Louis

He was nine when his mother died, and the Rainfords took him in.

Noble Glaudell was born on the 16th of February, 1907, in East St. Louis, Illinois — a railroad town across the river from St. Louis, rough and working-class and full of the noise of the yards.1 The weekend he was born was a fair one — the Post-Dispatch promised moderate temperatures and westerly winds.2

He was the second child of Lee Sebastian Glaudell and Cora Esther Rainford, who had married in December 1903.3 His older sister Winona had been born in 1905; his younger brother Raymond Lee would follow in 1915.4 The family name was rare — Glaudell, sometimes spelled Glaudel — and the family was still finding its footing between Missouri and Illinois, between St. Louis and Joliet, the way working people did in those years.5

His father left the Great War years one paper of his own. In September 1918, registering for the draft at Joliet, Lee gave his birth as the 16th of February, 1882 — his own statement of it, and a stronger witness than the 1890 his death certificate would one day claim — and wrote down as his nearest kin his brother Noble, at 108 Baker Avenue in Joliet. That was Noble August, the elder of the family's Nobles, Lee's brother and the boy's great-uncle; Joliet held him in 1918, close enough to be the name a man left for whoever might need telling.6

His mother Cora died in July 1916, when Noble was nine.7 By 1920 he was living as the nephew of Walter Rainford — his mother's people taking in her boy.8 A studio portrait survives of Noble at sixteen, hair combed neat, a tie knotted careful, smiling the practiced smile of a young man having his picture taken.9

§3 Adeline

The first marriage made him a father twice over, and ended before the war took him.

On the 18th of January, 1934, Noble married Adeline Rosella Emma Minnie Schaal at Crown Point, Indiana — the Lake County seat, just across the state line, the way couples did when they wanted it done simply.12 She was twenty, a Kankakee County girl from the Schaal family of Bonfield.13 Their son Noble Lee was born that July; a second son, Donald Ray, followed in 1936.14

The marriage did not last. By about 1941 Noble and Adeline had divorced — the date is an estimate, and the page keeps it labeled one.15 Adeline married Oliver Sutton in Chicago in March 1942.16 That spring of 1943, before the Army took him overseas, Noble stood for a photograph with his boys on a Symerton street — the family as it was, in the last season it was whole. Noble went to war.

§4 The Signal Corps

The Army spelled his name wrong on the way in, and the morning reports spelled it three ways more — the serial number is what ties the trail together.

Noble served in the Second World War with the 3165th Signal Service Company.17 The American Battle Monuments Commission's WWII Memorial Registry lists him as a World War II veteran, hometown Reedsburg, Wisconsin, honored by Fuhrman-Finnegan Post 350.18 His draft card and enlistment record survive, and the unit's history is partly known.19

The morning reports have since told the story of his training. He appears first in September 1943 — at Fort Custer, Michigan, and at the Signal Corps replacement training center at Camp Crowder, Missouri, where he completed the Powerman course that November. In the new year he moved to Fort Monmouth, New Jersey, and then, at the end of February 1944, to the Holabird Signal Depot in Baltimore. That May the depot gave him twelve days' delay en route, with permission to visit Joliet — a last trip home — before he reported to Camp Beale, California, on 4 June. There his name went onto Transfer List 354, Shipment RB 161(a), one of a company of Signal Corps men held for overseas movement; and in the first days of September 1944, under Special Orders No. 4 of the Central Pacific Base Command, he was assigned and joined at APO 958 — Honolulu — among men drawn from the 3116th Signal Service Battalion. It was the road that carried him, in time, to the 3165th and Saipan.19

The name that hid the record

His enlistment hid for years behind a spelling. The Army's electronic file holds him as GLAUDELL#NOBEL — first name NOBEL, not NOBLE — so every search for 'Noble Glaudell' walked past him, and one early pass through the records set the matching file aside as another man. What settled it was the serial number: 16144568, the same on the enlistment record, the headstone application, and the morning reports. Where the name varies, the number does not.

What the company itself was doing at the end of the war, its own papers now say. Month by month through the summer of 1945 the 3165th filed its war-diary report on its own letterhead — "3165th Signal Service Company, APO 244," and APO 244 was Saipan: the company ran the island's Joint Communication Center and its "Walnut" switchboard, kept up the wire plant of the whole island, and tended the submarine cable. A secret Central Pacific Base Command station list of 15 September 1945 counts the company on Saipan at twenty officers and two hundred forty-five enlisted men. But these are the company's pages, not his: Noble is named on none of them, and what his own hands did in the 3165th — beyond the specialty the Camp Beale roster numbers 506 — and whether his own feet ever stood on Saipan, the record does not say.20

§5 June

The second marriage lasted twenty years, and the family it made is the one that kept his papers.

In October 1952 — the 3rd, by Ancestry's reading of the Cook County index; the 4th, by FamilySearch's — Noble married again: June Margareta Hennings, in Cook County, Illinois.2122 She was twenty-seven, born in Hennepin, Minnesota; he was forty-five.23 That Christmas they were photographed at her mother's house — Noble in a bow tie, June smiling beside him, the two of them easy together.24 The wedding day was cool and dry — a high of 64 and no rain — and the next morning a cold front swept into Chicago, the high falling to 56 under northwest winds.29

They settled in Madison, Wisconsin, in an upstairs apartment at 4649 Tonyawatha Trail, with Lake Monona just behind the house, down two flights.25 A son, Dale Eric, was born in June 1959.26 The 1950 census had caught Noble in Chicago; by the mid-sixties the Madison papers were noting his bowling league and a real-estate transaction — the small print of an ordinary life.27 A snapshot from those years shows him in a boat, stretched out with a magazine, a handwritten caption declaring: "This is the way to fish."28

§6 Reedsburg

He came to Reedsburg in 1956, and Reedsburg is where the record of him closes — all but two answers, which are still in the mail or in an unmailed envelope.

Noble died on the 5th of August, 1972, in Reedsburg, Sauk County, Wisconsin, at a neighbor's home, of an apparent heart attack — in the town the Legion post called his hometown.30 The Capital Times and the Wisconsin State Journal both carried his obituary on the 7th of August.31 The day after he died, the Journal promised Reedsburg thundershowers, a high near 78, and a low of 60.32

He had come to Reedsburg in 1956. There he worked as a U.S. government inspector at Gibbs Manufacturing in Janesville, bowled in the local leagues — a 569 series made the Janesville paper in October 197133 — and belonged to the Reedsburg VFW.

The bronze tablet over him at Green Wood was June's doing. In the autumn after his death she applied for his government headstone herself — June M. Glaudell, widow, of 145 South Grove in Reedsburg, an address of hers the family papers had not held before — her signature dated the 2nd of October, 1972, asking for the flat bronze marker with its Latin cross; the marker was ordered the next July, cast by the Sheidow Bronze works. The application carries a whole paper life in miniature: his name first typed "Nobel," then corrected by hand to NOBLE — the same wrong spelling his enlistment record bore in 1942 — and two of the government's own records at odds over his discharge, the card saying the 28th of October, 1945, the BIRLS file the 28th of November, a month apart and not yet reconciled.34

June lived on until August 1998, dying in Reedsburg as well.35 They rest in the town that claimed him — his grave marked, hers not yet found.

His granddaughter Dana — who never knew him as anything but "Grandpa" in the family stories — would one day write that she and her husband were the unofficial Glaudell historians, tracing the line back to Alsace-Lorraine.36 The paper trail she inherited starts here: a boy from East St. Louis, a signalman, a fisherman, married twice, father of three, who ended his days by the lakes and hills of Sauk County.

Since this story was first written, the record has been mended in the places where the world's memory of him is kept: in October 2026 the corrections gathered in these pages were carried onto both family trees — seven on FamilySearch, ten on Ancestry — so that his parents, his wives, his sons, and his service stand there as the records give them. Two answers are still outstanding: the morning reports of January through October 1945 were never digitized, and NARA St. Louis answered in October 2026 that they can be had only in its research room or through an independent researcher — while his personnel file, the one record that could settle the discharge month, stands requested in a packet prepared and not yet mailed. And the Cook County Clerk holds the certified marriage record that will fix his and June's wedding day. When those come, this page will take in what they say.37

The proof trail

His open questions are not mysteries; they are unread records and unmailed packets. Each one below names the instrument that answers it.

The enlistment that wasn't his — until the number matched

The Fold3 pass of October 2025 found a WWII enlistment for a 'Nobel Glaudell,' born 1910 in the Panama Canal Zone, and set it aside as another man. The NARA AAD file — the Army's own electronic enlistment record — holds the same man under the spelling GLAUDELL#NOBEL, with birth year 1907, nativity Illinois, Will County residence, enlistment 23 October 1942 at Chicago, Signal Corps, 'divorced with dependents' — and serial number 16144568, identical to the set-aside record. The exclusion was rescinded: the birth fields in the Fold3 snippet were the error; the serial was the man.

The parentage proof

For years his parents rested on the tree alone. The census images read in October 2026 settled it: in 1910 Noble, age 3, stands as SON in Lee and Cora Glaudell's St. Louis household, and in 1920 he stands as nephew in his mother's brother's household in Wesley Township. Two independent enumerations, sixteen years of family memory confirmed — the grade rose to Proven.

The morning-report trail

The NARA Catalog series 'Morning Reports, 1940–1946' was searched by serial number alone — the only discriminator the name's spellings allow. Twenty-four file units answered, dated September 1943 to September 1944; forty-two verified pages naming him were copied and filed. The digitized run ends in December 1944. For 1945 the lane wrote to NARA St. Louis; the reply of 16 October 2026 closed the mail route — the reports are obtainable only in the NPRC Archival Research Room or through an independent researcher.

Two VA records, one month apart

The VA's BIRLS death file gives his release from service as 28 November 1945. June's headstone application — also a VA record — types 28 October 1945. Neither outranks the other, and this page does not choose. The decider is his Official Military Personnel File at NPRC; the request packet stands prepared in the family's mail queue, unmailed.

Where was the first wedding?

The tree pins the 1934 marriage to Point Township, Posey County, Indiana, citing a FamilySearch index entry — an entry no one in the lane has read at image grade. Adeline's lane holds the marriage license itself: Lake County, Indiana, Marriage Records 1933–34, married at Crown Point by the Rev. August Biester. This page follows the record that has been seen; the Posey citation stands in the ledger as an open conflict until its image is read.

Which day in October?

Two indexes of the same Cook County marriage disagree by a day: Ancestry reads the 3rd of October 1952, FamilySearch the 4th; his obituary says only '1951.' The register image is not viewable online; the certified copy from the Cook County Clerk is the decider, and its request packet stands prepared.
